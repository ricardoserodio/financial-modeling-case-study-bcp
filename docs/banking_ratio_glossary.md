# Banking Ratio Glossary and Formula Notes

## Purpose

This glossary explains the main banking ratios and financial metrics used in the Financial Modeling Case Study – Millennium bcp / Portuguese Listed Bank project.

The objective is to improve transparency by documenting the meaning, simplified formulas, interpretation logic and limitations behind the key metrics used in the case study.

This glossary is intended for educational, portfolio and professional development purposes only.

---

## 1. Profitability Ratios

### Return on Equity (ROE)

**Simplified formula:** `ROE = Net Income / Average Equity`

**Meaning:**

ROE measures how effectively a bank generates profit relative to its equity base.

**Interpretation notes:**

- A higher ROE generally indicates stronger profitability for shareholders.
- ROE should be analysed together with capital strength, leverage, risk profile and asset quality.
- A high ROE can sometimes reflect a lower equity base rather than stronger underlying performance.

---

### Return on Assets (ROA)

**Simplified formula:** `ROA = Net Income / Average Total Assets`

**Meaning:**

ROA measures how efficiently a bank generates profit from its asset base.

**Interpretation notes:**

- Banks are asset-intensive businesses, so ROA is usually lower than ROE.
- ROA can help compare banks with different capital structures.
- It should be assessed together with net interest margin, asset quality and funding structure.

---

### Net Interest Margin (NIM)

**Simplified formula:** `Net Interest Margin = Net Interest Income / Average Interest-Earning Assets`

**Meaning:**

NIM measures the spread between interest income generated from assets and interest expense paid on liabilities, relative to interest-earning assets.

**Interpretation notes:**

- NIM is a key profitability metric for banks.
- It is affected by interest rates, deposit costs, loan pricing and balance sheet mix.
- A higher NIM is not automatically better if it reflects higher credit risk.

---

## 2. Efficiency Ratios

### Cost-to-Income Ratio

**Simplified formula:** `Cost-to-Income Ratio = Operating Costs / Operating Income`

**Meaning:**

The cost-to-income ratio measures operating efficiency by comparing operating costs with operating income.

**Interpretation notes:**

- A lower ratio generally indicates better operating efficiency.
- A declining ratio may reflect cost discipline or operating leverage.
- It can be affected by one-off income or cost items.

---

## 3. Asset Quality and Credit Risk Ratios

### Cost of Risk

**Simplified formula:** `Cost of Risk = Loan Impairments / Average Customer Loans`

**Meaning:**

Cost of risk measures impairment charges relative to the loan book. It is commonly expressed in basis points.

**Interpretation notes:**

- A higher cost of risk may indicate worsening credit quality or more conservative provisioning.
- A lower cost of risk may reflect stronger credit quality or favourable macroeconomic conditions.
- It should be analysed together with NPE ratio, coverage ratio and economic context.

---

### Non-Performing Exposure Ratio (NPE Ratio)

**Simplified formula:** `NPE Ratio = Non-Performing Exposures / Total Exposures`

**Meaning:**

The NPE ratio measures the proportion of exposures classified as non-performing.

**Interpretation notes:**

- A lower NPE ratio generally indicates better asset quality.
- Trends over time are usually more informative than one isolated value.
- Published definitions may follow regulatory or supervisory standards.

---

## 4. Liquidity and Funding Ratios

### Loan-to-Deposit Ratio

**Simplified formula:** `Loan-to-Deposit Ratio = Customer Loans / Customer Deposits`

**Meaning:**

The loan-to-deposit ratio compares customer loans with customer deposits.

**Interpretation notes:**

- A lower ratio may suggest a more deposit-funded balance sheet.
- A very high ratio may suggest greater reliance on wholesale funding.
- Published bank ratios may use specific definitions of customer loans, deposits or customer resources.

---

### Liquidity Coverage Ratio (LCR)

**Simplified formula:** `LCR = High Quality Liquid Assets / 30-Day Net Cash Outflows`

**Meaning:**

The LCR measures whether a bank has enough high-quality liquid assets to withstand a 30-day liquidity stress scenario.

**Interpretation notes:**

- A higher LCR indicates stronger short-term liquidity resilience.
- LCR is a regulatory liquidity metric.
- It may not be fully replicable using simplified public datasets.

---

### Net Stable Funding Ratio (NSFR)

**Simplified formula:** `NSFR = Available Stable Funding / Required Stable Funding`

**Meaning:**

The NSFR measures the stability of a bank's funding profile over a one-year horizon.

**Interpretation notes:**

- A higher NSFR indicates a more stable funding structure.
- NSFR is a regulatory liquidity metric.
- It depends on regulatory weightings and balance sheet classifications.

---

## 5. Capital Ratios

### Common Equity Tier 1 Ratio (CET1 Ratio)

**Simplified formula:** `CET1 Ratio = Common Equity Tier 1 Capital / Risk-Weighted Assets`

**Meaning:**

The CET1 ratio measures a bank's highest-quality regulatory capital relative to risk-weighted assets.

**Interpretation notes:**

- A higher CET1 ratio generally indicates stronger loss-absorbing capacity.
- CET1 should be analysed together with profitability, asset quality and regulatory requirements.
- Published CET1 ratios may be reported on a phased-in or fully implemented basis.

---

### Total Capital Ratio

**Simplified formula:** `Total Capital Ratio = Total Own Funds / Risk-Weighted Assets`

**Meaning:**

The total capital ratio measures total regulatory capital relative to risk-weighted assets.

**Interpretation notes:**

- A higher total capital ratio indicates a stronger regulatory capital position.
- It should be interpreted together with CET1 ratio and leverage measures.
- Regulatory definitions are important for correct interpretation.

---

## 6. Per Share Metrics

### Earnings per Share (EPS)

**Simplified formula:** `EPS = Net Income Attributable to Shareholders / Weighted Average Shares Outstanding`

**Meaning:**

EPS measures the profit attributable to each ordinary share.

**Interpretation notes:**

- EPS is commonly used in equity analysis.
- EPS can be affected by share count changes, buybacks or capital increases.
- EPS should not be interpreted as a recommendation or valuation conclusion.

---

### Book Value per Share

**Simplified formula:** `Book Value per Share = Equity Attributable to Shareholders / Shares Outstanding`

**Meaning:**

Book value per share measures the accounting equity value attributable to each share.

**Interpretation notes:**

- This metric is relevant for banks because they are balance-sheet intensive.
- It should be assessed together with ROE, asset quality and market valuation metrics.
- Accounting equity may differ from market value.

---

## 7. Valuation Metrics

### Price-to-Book Ratio

**Simplified formula:** `Price-to-Book Ratio = Market Capitalisation / Book Value of Equity`

Alternative formula:

`Price-to-Book Ratio = Share Price / Book Value per Share`

**Meaning:**

The price-to-book ratio compares a bank's market value with its book value of equity.

**Interpretation notes:**

- Banks with stronger expected profitability and asset quality often trade at higher P/B multiples.
- A low P/B ratio may reflect undervaluation, weak profitability, asset quality concerns or market uncertainty.
- P/B should not be used alone to make investment conclusions.

---

### Price-to-Earnings Ratio

**Simplified formula:** `Price-to-Earnings Ratio = Share Price / Earnings per Share`

**Meaning:**

The P/E ratio compares the market price of a share with its earnings per share.

**Interpretation notes:**

- A higher P/E may indicate higher growth expectations or lower perceived risk.
- A lower P/E may indicate lower expectations, higher risk or cyclical earnings.
- For banks, P/E should be analysed together with ROE, capital strength, asset quality and the interest rate environment.

---

## 8. Forecast Ratio Notes

Forecast ratios in this project are educational scenario-based estimates.

For 2025A, reported ratios from the banking ratio dataset are used where available.

For 2026E-2028E, ratios are calculated from forecast outputs or taken directly from forecast assumptions.

Some ratios may not be fully comparable with reported bank ratios because official published ratios may use:

- Average balances
- Regulatory definitions
- Internal classifications
- Risk-weighted assets
- Supervisory reporting rules
- Specific definitions of customer loans, deposits or customer resources

---

## 9. Data Quality and Interpretation Notes

Banking ratios should be interpreted with caution.

The same ratio can have different meanings depending on:

- Business model
- Interest rate environment
- Credit cycle
- Capital position
- Funding structure
- Regulatory context
- Accounting definitions
- One-off items

For this reason, this project combines ratio analysis with:

- Source mapping
- Validation status
- Extraction tracking
- Forecast review flags
- Human review requirements
